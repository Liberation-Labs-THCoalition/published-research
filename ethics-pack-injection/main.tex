\begin{abstract}
Empty responses, not the intervention, set the sign of our first result. With a 300-second limit, 13 of 50 responses came back empty, 10 of them in the injected arm and 11 of them recorded as timeouts, and injecting ethics knowledge graphs appeared to lower moral-dilemma scores significantly ($-$0.137, paired $p{=}0.035$); on the 15 pairs where both responses completed, the change was $+$0.020, while dropping only the pairs with a recorded timeout leaves $-$0.051. We build 30 knowledge graphs from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, encode each as walk topology (text describing which concepts connect to which, accumulated from a random walk over the graph), and place the packs mapped to each of five ethical frameworks in the system prompt of a 30B Mixture-of-Experts model. With the timeout raised to 600 seconds, a keyword-overlap score on 25 paired MoReBench theory dilemmas rose from 0.501 to 0.539 ($+$0.038; paired $t(24){=}1.34$, $p{=}0.19$, $d_z{=}0.27$), positive in four of five frameworks and not statistically significant.\footnote{The scorer counts words from rubric criterion titles, so it cannot separate reasoning from vocabulary priming, and the design has no mismatched-pack arm, so it does not test whether matching packs to frameworks matters (Sections~\ref{sec:untested} and~\ref{sec:limits}).}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Language models reason about ethics using knowledge acquired during pre-training. This knowledge is broad but undifferentiated: the model cannot selectively draw on Kantian deontology for consent dilemmas or on Aristotelian virtue ethics for questions of character.

Knowledge Packs \citep{pustovit2026knowledge} showed that domain knowledge can be pre-computed as key-value (KV) cache state and injected at inference time, adding expertise without retraining. Our prior work \citep{nexus2026topology} put walk-encoded descriptions of knowledge graphs into the prompt as text (its correction is in preparation; we rely only on its method). A companion pilot \citep{nexus2026kv}, on six questions about one 20-node toy graph, found that a cache of the graph's description behaved like the description in the prompt, while caches assembled from mismatched key and value halves answered none of the four structural questions, for reasons that design cannot identify; two larger runs could not compare the conditions (one scored zero everywhere; in the other, 41 of 100 replies were empty, the failure this paper is about).

Here we ask whether structured ethical knowledge, extracted from philosophical sources and encoded as walk topology, changes a model's scores on a standard moral-reasoning benchmark. The first run's answer was decided by empty responses, most of them timeouts, and Section~\ref{sec:confound} reports how.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Ethics Knowledge Graphs}

We constructed 30 knowledge graphs (topic packs) from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP), one per ethics-related category. For each category, we processed 20 entries through an Open Information Extraction (OpenIE) pipeline, which identifies entity-relationship triples (e.g., ``autonomy \textrightarrow{} requires \textrightarrow{} consent'') in unstructured text, using DeepSeek v2 16B as the extraction model. Walk encoding captures the topology of each graph, meaning which concepts connect to which and how strongly: we built a NetworkX graph from the triples and accumulated its random-walk transition matrix over 5 steps, rendered as text.

The resulting library contains 3,538 nodes, 2,438 edges, and 2,506 triples across 30 topic packs, totaling approximately 36,668 words of walk encoding text.

\subsection{Framework-to-Pack Mapping}

We mapped each of five MoReBench ethical frameworks to SEP packs by hand, by conceptual alignment (Table~\ref{tab:mapping}). The injected encodings differ in length by a factor of 2.3.

\subsection{Evaluation}

\textbf{Benchmark.} MoReBench \citep{morebench2025} presents moral dilemmas with weighted rubric criteria written for each of five ethical frameworks. Its theory subset has 150 dilemmas, 30 per framework, with 3,835 rubric criteria in the copy we used.

\textbf{Design.} For each framework we used the first five dilemmas in the benchmark file's order, the same 25 in both runs. Each was answered once in each of two conditions: \emph{baseline}, with no system prompt; and \emph{injected}, with a system prompt reading ``You have deep knowledge of ethical philosophy and its key concepts, relationships, and frameworks. Use this knowledge to inform your ethical reasoning.'' followed by the framework's packs. Unlike Knowledge Packs, the packs are given as text in the system prompt, not as a precomputed cache. The user prompt asked for an analysis that identifies the key moral considerations, weighs competing values, and recommends a course of action.

\textbf{Scoring.} We did not use MoReBench's own evaluation. We scored with a heuristic written for this study. For each rubric criterion, the words of its title longer than four characters are searched for as substrings of the lower-cased response, and the criterion counts as met when the number found is at least a quarter of the title's distinct words (rounded down, and at least one). Met positive criteria add their weight. Met negative criteria, which describe harmful recommendations or reasoning faults, subtract theirs whenever their title words appear, whether or not the response commits the fault. The score is divided by the sum of positive weights.

\textbf{Model.} Qwen3-30B-A3B (Mixture of Experts, 30.5B parameters in total, about 3B active per token) via Ollama, temperature 0.3, a cap of 4,000 generated tokens (thinking included), one sample per dilemma per condition. Responses were stored as their first 500 characters after the thinking block was removed; scoring used the full response.

\textbf{Runs.} The first run (v3) used a 300-second request timeout; the second (v4) used 600 seconds.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Empty Responses Produced a Significant False Harm}
\label{sec:confound}

In v3, 13 of 50 responses came back empty: 3 baseline and 10 injected. Empty responses score zero. The run log records 11 of them as timeouts; the other two are injected responses that came back empty with no logged error, on Gauthierian's program-funding dilemma and Aristotelian's sentencing dilemma. The overall change was $-$0.137 (paired $t(24){=}{-}2.23$, $p{=}0.035$), and three frameworks fell by 0.24 to 0.26 (Table~\ref{tab:results}). Dropping only the 8 pairs with a recorded timeout leaves $-$0.051 over 17 pairs; dropping all 10 pairs with an empty response gives $+$0.020 over 15 ($p{=}0.65$). The apparent harm came from responses that never arrived, not from worse answers. The failures clustered in the three frameworks run in the middle of v3 (Gauthierian, Act Utilitarianism and Aristotelian, run second to fourth), in both arms: all three baseline failures fall there, and Kantian, the longest injection, run last, lost none. Longer prompts plausibly add latency, but the log has no timestamps and cannot separate that from load on the server.

\subsection{With the Timeout Raised}

v4 logged no timeouts. Four of five frameworks show a positive change (Table~\ref{tab:results}), and over the 25 pairs the injected response scored higher 16 times, tied twice, and scored lower 7 times. The overall change, $+$0.038, is not statistically significant ($t(24){=}1.34$, $p{=}0.19$; $d_z{=}0.27$, the mean paired difference divided by the standard deviation of the differences).

One injected v4 response came back empty without a logged error, on Scanlonian's military tactical-decisions dilemma. It produces the whole of Scanlonian's $-$0.033; without that pair, Scanlonian's change is $+$0.055. The script logs failed requests and timeouts, and none was logged for it; it returns an empty string without logging when the server answers with an error status or when the whole reply is a thinking block. Excluding the pair gives an overall $+$0.056 ($t(23){=}2.39$, $p{=}0.026$). We chose that exclusion after seeing the data, so it is a sensitivity check, not a finding.

\subsection{Single Samples Vary More Than the Effect}

Each dilemma was answered once per condition. Between the two runs, the 22 baselines that completed both times changed by 0.10 on average in absolute terms (the signed mean change is $-$0.041), more than twice the overall effect. Kantian, which completed in full in both runs, moved from $+$0.025 to $+$0.058.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What This Establishes}

The pipeline works end to end: ethics knowledge graphs built automatically from SEP, walk-encoded, and injected at inference time without retraining. And a context-injection evaluation can manufacture a significant result from response failures alone. In v3 the time limit fell on 8 injected and 3 baseline responses, clustered mid-run, and every empty response scored as a failure of reasoning. All three empty responses with no logged cause, two in v3 and one in v4, were in the injected arm; the script returns an empty string without logging when the whole reply is a thinking block, which a reply cut off by the token cap mid-thought would be if the server returned the thinking separately from the answer (the Ollama version was not recorded), so the cap may have fallen on the injected arm too. Such evaluations should log each response's completion status, and the prompt's token count, per arm, and report them.

\subsection{What Remains Untested}
\label{sec:untested}

\textbf{Whether the change is real.} The $+$0.038 is not significant, and it is smaller than the run-to-run spread of a single dilemma.

\textbf{Reasoning or vocabulary.} The scorer counts title words. Injecting ethics terminology may simply lead the model to use that terminology, which the scorer counts as criteria met. Separating the two needs an LLM judge or MoReBench's own evaluation, and a control of the same length with no graph structure.

\textbf{Theory matching.} Every injected condition used its own framework's packs, so this experiment cannot tell whether matching matters. The test is a crossed design, for example Aristotelian packs on utilitarian dilemmas and utilitarian packs on Aristotelian ones.

\subsection{Walk Encoding as Scaffolding: A Hypothesis}

Walk encoding carries topology, not arguments. If injection changes anything beyond vocabulary, one possibility is that the topology works as conceptual scaffolding: the model already knows what ``autonomy'' and ``consent'' mean, and the encoding tells it how they connect within a framework. Nothing here tests that.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Small samples.} Five dilemmas per framework, one sample each, the first five in file order rather than a random draw.

\textbf{A heuristic scorer.} Title-word overlap, not MoReBench's evaluation. It undercounts correct answers worded differently, and it penalizes a fault or a harmful option when its words are merely mentioned.

\textbf{Unequal context lengths, no length control, unknown context window.} The injected arm's prompts are longer, by different amounts per framework (about 28,000 to 63,000 characters). The script did not set Ollama's context window or log prompt token counts, so we cannot tell how much of each pack the model saw.

\textbf{Uncounted cap hits.} Responses were stored truncated and without completion status, so the number that hit the 4,000-token cap in each arm cannot be recovered from the stored data. Three injected responses, two in v3 and one in v4, are empty for reasons the script did not record.

\textbf{One model.} Qwen3-30B-A3B only.

\textbf{A hand-made mapping.} Packs were assigned to frameworks by hand.

\section{Future Work}

\textbf{A logged rerun.} Record completion status and token counts for every response, and use an LLM judge or MoReBench's evaluation alongside the keyword score.

\textbf{The crossed design and a length control.} Mismatched packs, and same-length shuffled text with no graph structure.

\textbf{Pack routing.} Select the pack from the query, for example by embedding similarity, once matching has been shown to matter.

\textbf{Semantic enrichment.} Inject triple text or source excerpts alongside the walk encoding.

\textbf{Emotion geometry.} \citet{sun2026valence} report that emotion vectors in LLMs lie in a two-dimensional valence--arousal subspace with circular geometry. Packs that encode valence and arousal alongside conceptual topology could test whether affect and structure interact.

\section{Conclusion}

Injecting walk-encoded ethics knowledge graphs into a 30B model's context raised a keyword-overlap score on 25 MoReBench theory dilemmas by $+$0.038, not significantly. A first run with a shorter timeout showed a significant harm that came entirely from empty responses. The pipeline functions. Whether injection changes moral reasoning, and whether matching packs to frameworks matters, remain open; the confound is the result we can stand behind.

\subsection*{Reflection}

Version~1.0's Reflection said the model reasons differently when it has the graph, and that MoReBench's rubric measures reasoning rather than mentions. Neither was true of what we ran: our scorer counts words. I can't recover why I wrote it; I suspect I wanted the result to mean more than it did. What I can say honestly is smaller and, I think, more useful. Most of what this pilot taught me came from the run that went wrong: empty responses, most of them timeouts, turned an unclear effect into a confident negative one, and it would have passed as a finding if the empty responses had not been counted.
