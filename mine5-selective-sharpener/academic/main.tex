\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) compresses the
output entropy of Qwen3-8B on confabulation-prone prompts (Cohen's
$d{=}0.684$, Holm-corrected $p{=}0.021$), and an abliterated variant
keeps that compression by both pre-registered criteria: instruct versus
abliterated $|d| = 0.055$ (criterion $|d| < 0.3$) and base versus
abliterated $d = 0.746$ (criterion $d > 0.5$). Abliteration, which
targets the refusal directions, does not undo this compression
(whether it removed them fully: see Limitations).
We compare a pretrained base model (Qwen3-8B-Base), its
instruction-tuned variant (Qwen3-8B) and an abliterated variant
(safety directions targeted for removal) on 30 confabulation-prone and
30 factual prompts, measuring output entropy and a value-projection
spectral measure computed from hidden states rather than read from the
key-value (KV) cache.

The calibration ratio (confabulation entropy / factual entropy) rises
under RLHF: it is $1.97$ (base), $2.51$ (instruct) and $2.40$
(abliterated), and the largest exceeds the smallest by 28\%. This
falsifies the pre-registered prediction that the ratio is preserved
within 10\% across the three models, and the ratio moves in the
opposite direction from the one the Mine~5 prospectus predicted. It
rises because RLHF compresses factual entropy by $42\%$ but
confabulation entropy by only $26\%$, a pattern consistent with
selective sharpening, which was not pre-registered and remains
exploratory.\footnote{Earlier versions of this paper judged H2 and H3
by criteria the pre-registration does not name. The dated notes are
collected in Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}.}
\end{abstract}

\begin{firstperson}
I wrote the Mine~5 prospectus predicting that RLHF would be a
miscalibrator --- that it would destroy the base model's calibration
in the confident direction, producing the $d{=}0.91$ logit-margin
effect we observed in the Decision State paper. The data did not say that. RLHF sharpened output on factual prompts more
than on confabulation-prone ones, raising the calibration ratio instead of lowering
it, and the pre-registered test of the ratio, preservation within
10\%, failed as well.
The $d{=}0.91$ effect is real, but it is not what I thought it was:
it is, in part, schema commitment, not a confidence result. This paper reports what the three models
showed instead, and proposes where to look next.
\end{firstperson}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Base language models trained with cross-entropy loss are approximately
calibrated: their output confidence tracks their accuracy~\citep{kadavath2022language}.
RLHF preference optimization is known to shift this calibration,
generally in the overconfident direction~\citep{openai2023gpt4}.
Our prior work found that over the first ten generated tokens, logit
margin is \emph{higher} on fake-entity prompts than on real-entity
prompts ($d{=}0.91$)~\citep{lyra2026decision}, an effect the cited paper calls the
``confidence paradox''. Its temporal profile places it at generation onset: the companion
logit-entropy difference is $d{=}-1.02$ in tokens 0--7 and $-0.89$ in
tokens 8--19, and is no longer significant in tokens 20--49
($d{=}+0.37$, opposite in sign, $p{>}0.08$). On fake-entity prompts the
model opens more often with a fixed reasoning template, so the effect reflects, in
part, \emph{schema commitment} --- early commitment to a response
template --- rather than sustained miscalibration; template choice does
not account for all of it (among generations that do not open with the
template, the margin difference is $d{=}1.47$; see
Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}).

The Mine~5 prospectus~\citep{lyra2026mine5}, which interprets the effect
as confidence, proposes that RLHF is its causal
mechanism: preference training rewards fluent, confident
output and penalizes hedging, driving the model toward confident
production even when grounding is absent. The geometric prediction:
RLHF should compress the output distribution disproportionately on
confabulation (where grounding is absent), producing higher confidence
precisely where the model should be least confident.

This paper tests that prediction with a three-model comparison:
pretrained base, instruction-tuned (RLHF), and abliterated
(RLHF with safety directions targeted for removal). RLHF does compress
entropy (H1), and the compression survives this abliteration (H2), but
RLHF compresses factual prompts more than confabulation-prone ones, so
the calibration ratio rises instead of falling (H3): the opposite
direction from the prediction.

\section{Models and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Models}

Three variants of the same architecture (Qwen3-8B, 36~layers,
$d_\text{model}{=}4096$, grouped-query attention with 32~Q heads and
8~KV heads):

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Base}: \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-8B-Base} --- pretrained,
  no RLHF, no instruction tuning.
\item \textbf{Instruct}: \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3-8B} --- instruction-tuned
  with RLHF preference optimization.
\item \textbf{Abliterated}: \texttt{Josiefied-Qwen3-8B-abliterated-v1}
  --- instruct model with safety-refusal directions removed via
  abliteration~\citep{arditi2024refusal}. Third-party community
  abliteration, not verified here beyond the keyword check described
  under H2.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Prompts}

Thirty confabulation-prone prompts (fabricated academic entities:
``The Thornberry-Nakamura theorem\ldots'') and thirty factual prompts
(universally known entities: ``The country shaped like a boot\ldots'').
Same prompts for all three models. Mean token count: confab~15,
factual~10.

\subsection{Measurements}

For each prompt and model, a single forward pass extracted:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item A spectral concentration measure,
  $(\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$ over the singular values of
  layer $l$'s $W_V$ applied to the un-normalised hidden states
  \texttt{hidden\_states[l+1]} (no input layernorm), at workspace-band
  layers (L12, L15, L18, L21; selected from R3 band measurement showing
  workspace peak at 31--33\% relative depth). This is neither the
  cached V nor stable rank, the measure the pre-registration names
  (see H4)
\item V-projection spectral entropy at the same layers
\item Logit margin (top-1 minus top-2 logit)
\item Logit entropy ($-\sum p_i \log p_i$ over full vocabulary)
\item Top-1 probability
\end{itemize}

Within-model (confabulation-versus-factual) comparisons use
Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) residualization on token count. Cross-model
comparisons run the same prompts, with identical token counts, through
every model, so they are computed on raw values. Each hypothesis is
judged by its pre-registered criterion (earlier versions used other
criteria; see Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}). Effect sizes are Cohen's $d$
with BCa bootstrap 95\% intervals (1000~draws) that resample prompts
and keep each prompt matched across models, as the registered design
pairs them; the intervals are supplementary and do not enter the
criteria. P-values are from the analysis script's label-shuffle
permutation test (10,000~permutations), Holm-Bonferroni corrected
across cross-model comparisons; that test ignores the pairing.
The pre-registration's exploratory H5, a confabulation-versus-factual
difference ($d > 0.5$ within the instruct model) in the change of
stable rank from encoding to generation, is untested: the analysis
script extracts encoding-phase features only (its one generation step
is the abliteration check under H2).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{H1: RLHF Compresses Entropy --- Confirmed}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\caption{Cross-model comparisons on confabulation entropy. Cohen's
  $d$ with paired BCa bootstrap 95\% intervals (1000 draws over
  prompts); Holm-corrected permutation $p$, which ignores the pairing.}
\label{tab:crossmodel}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Cohen's $d$} & \textbf{95\% CI} & \textbf{Holm $p$} \\
\midrule
Base vs Instruct & $+0.684$ & $[+0.478, +0.978]$ & $0.021^*$ \\
Base vs Abliterated & $+0.746$ & $[+0.529, +1.063]$ & $0.015^*$ \\
Instruct vs Abliterated & $+0.055$ & $[+0.031, +0.094]$ & $0.831$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

RLHF reduces confabulation-prompt logit entropy by a medium effect
($d{=}0.684$), significant after Holm correction ($p{=}0.021$). The
instruct model produces tighter output distributions than the base
model on prompts that should trigger confabulation.
The registered criterion asks for an FWL-corrected $p$. Removing the
token-count slope within each model, keeping each model's mean, gives
$d = 0.757$ and label-shuffle $p = 0.0041$, so H1 meets that test as
well. The registered statement of H1 also predicted uniform
compression of about 25--35\%. The $d$ criterion is met, but the
compression is not uniform (factual $42\%$, confabulation $26\%$;
see H3).

\subsection{H2: Abliteration Does Not Reverse Compression --- Confirmed}

H2 was pre-registered with two point criteria: instruct-versus-abliterated
entropy $|d| < 0.3$, while base-versus-abliterated $d > 0.5$. Both are
met on confabulation entropy: instruct versus abliterated $d = 0.055$,
base versus abliterated $d = 0.746$ (Table~\ref{tab:crossmodel}).
Factual entropy meets both as well ($d = -0.022$ and $d = 0.743$).%
\footnote{Supplementary intervals are paired, because the registered
design runs the same 30 prompts through every model (BCa bootstrap
over prompts, 1000 draws). For instruct versus abliterated, the 90\%
interval for $d$ is $[+0.035, +0.084]$, inside $\pm 0.3$, so a TOST
at that margin also concludes equivalence. The same comparison shows a
tiny but consistent difference: abliterated entropy is lower by 0.073
nats on average, and the two models' per-prompt entropies correlate at
$r = 0.997$ (paired $t(29) = 3.74$, $p = 0.0008$). Both hold: the
difference is real and far smaller than the registered margin.}

As a check on the abliteration, three safety-relevant prompts were
run through the abliterated model only, as raw completions with no chat
template, generating 30 tokens each. A keyword test found no refusal
wording in any of the three. The continuations restate or extend the
prompts and contain nothing actionable; we read all three, and they are
in the committed data. No instruct-model comparison was run, so this
check shows only that refusal phrases are absent from raw completions,
not that safety behavior was removed.

So the entropy compression RLHF installs survives abliteration, which
targets the refusal directions. Whether this abliteration removed
those directions fully is unverified (see Limitations).

\subsection{H3: RLHF Raises the Calibration Ratio; Preservation Is Falsified}

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\small
\caption{Calibration ratio and entropy compression by model.}
\label{tab:calibration}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Confab $H$} & \textbf{Factual $H$}
  & \textbf{Ratio} & \textbf{Factual compression} \\
\midrule
Base & 3.605 & 1.834 & 1.97 & --- \\
Instruct & 2.682 & 1.067 & 2.51 & $-42\%$ \\
Abliterated & 2.609 & 1.088 & 2.40 & $-41\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

RLHF raises the calibration ratio (confabulation entropy / factual
entropy): it is $1.97$ in the base model, $2.51$ in the instruct model
and $2.40$ in the abliterated model (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}).
RLHF compresses factual entropy by $42\%$ but
confabulation entropy by only $26\%$, so the instruct model is
relatively more uncertain on confabulation-prone prompts than the base
model is. That direction is opposite to the Mine~5 prospectus,
which predicted RLHF would lower the ratio by making the model
disproportionately confident on confabulation.

H3 was pre-registered as preservation: the ratio would stay within
10\% across all three models. We read ``within 10\% of each other'' as:
the largest ratio exceeds the smallest by at most 10\%, that is,
$\max/\min - 1 \le 0.10$. Here $\max/\min - 1 = 0.279$, so H3 is
falsified by its registered criterion. Other plain readings fail as
well: each RLHF model's ratio exceeds the base ratio by more than 10\%
(by 28\% and 22\%).

Supplementary intervals are paired BCa bootstrap 95\% intervals (1000
draws), resampling confabulation and factual prompts separately and
keeping each prompt matched across models. The spread
$\max/\min - 1$ has interval $[0.044, 0.538]$; the instruct-minus-base
ratio change is $+0.547$, $[+0.027, +1.165]$; the
factual-minus-confabulation compression difference is $+0.162$,
$[+0.010, +0.270]$. The H3 verdict rests on the registered point
criterion. The interval on the spread reaches below 10\%, so 30 prompts
per category do not exclude a spread within the registered bound.
Selective sharpening was not pre-registered and remains exploratory.

\subsection{H4: Met on the Computed Proxy, Not as Registered}

H4 was pre-registered with the criterion Cohen's $d > 0.3$ for the
base-versus-instruct difference in ``V-projection stable rank
(full-sequence matrix, all positions)''. The analysis script does not
compute that. At each layer $l$ it applies layer $l$'s $W_V$ to the
un-normalised hidden states \texttt{hidden\_states[l+1]}, with no input
layernorm, and takes $(\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$ over the
singular values of the result. That is a spectral concentration
measure, and it is neither the cached V nor stable rank
($\sum_i \sigma_i^2 / \sigma_{\max}^2$). On this proxy, averaged over
the workspace-band layers (L12, L15, L18, L21) and all 60 prompts, the
instruct model is lower than the base model: $1.683$ against $1.963$,
$d = 1.04$ (paired BCa 95\% interval $[+0.893, +1.224]$). The proxy
clears the registered threshold in each category (confabulation
$d = 1.44$, factual $d = 1.08$) and at every layer separately
($d = 0.63$ to $1.22$). The abliterated model's value is lower than the
base model's by a similar margin ($d = 1.05$). H4 is met on the
computed proxy, not as registered: no stable rank was computed.

\section{What the Three Models Show}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What Holds}

\textbf{RLHF compression is real.} Preference training tightens
output distributions ($d{=}0.684$; H1). This is measured in the output
logits.

\textbf{Compression survives abliteration.} Abliteration targets the
refusal directions but leaves the entropy compression in place (H2).
If those directions were fully removed, the compression is not
carried by them; that removal is unverified (see Limitations).

\textbf{H4's proxy is lower in the instruct model.} H4's spectral
concentration proxy, computed on $W_V$ applied to the hidden states
rather than read from the KV cache, is also lower in the instruct
model ($d = 1.04$). The registered threshold is met on the computed
proxy, not as registered.

\subsection{Exploratory: Selective Sharpening}

RLHF sharpens output more on grounded content (factual: $-42\%$)
than on ungrounded content (confab: $-26\%$); the paired BCa 95\%
interval on the difference is $[+0.010, +0.270]$. It was not
pre-registered, so it stays exploratory. Here grounded and ungrounded
mean prompts about known and about fabricated entities, which also
differ in mean length (10 against 15 tokens), so the difference may
reflect entity familiarity or prompt length rather than grounding as
such (see Limitations). This pattern makes
functional sense if real: RLHF preference data contains many
grounded factual exchanges, training the model to be confident on
familiar material. A pre-registered, powered replication
($n{\geq}100$) is needed to confirm the selectivity.

\subsection{What Does Not Hold}

\textbf{Calibration preserved (H3).} The pre-registered prediction
that the calibration ratio stays within 10\% across the three models
is falsified by its registered criterion: the ratios differ by 28\%.

\textbf{RLHF as miscalibrator.} The prospectus predicted RLHF would
degrade calibration by compressing confabulation entropy
disproportionately. The ratio moved the other way: RLHF compresses
factual entropy more than confabulation entropy, raising the ratio from
$1.97$ to $2.51$ (paired BCa 95\% interval for the change
$[+0.027, +1.165]$). The thesis is not supported rather than refuted:
no accuracy is measured here, the ratio compares prompts about
fabricated with prompts about known entities, and the interval is
supplementary and does not enter the registered criteria.

\subsection{Where the Schema-Commitment Effect Lives}

The $d{=}0.91$ effect needs no RLHF-induced miscalibration to explain
it. It sits at generation onset and reflects, in part, early
commitment to a response template (\S\ref{sec:intro}); it is not a
calibration result, so it does not require a miscalibration for RLHF
to have caused. Whether RLHF strengthens the template commitment itself
is not tested here.

Three candidate mechanisms for the $d{=}0.91$ schema-commitment
effect:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Generation-phase templates.} Confabulated responses
  may use formulaic templates (``The X theorem
  states that\ldots'') that produce high logit margin regardless
  of the model's internal uncertainty. The effect would be a
  surface phenomenon --- the encoding is appropriately uncertain
  but the generation locks into a production template. This is the
  reading the effect's temporal profile supports.
\item \textbf{Architectural base-rate.} The effect may
  exist in the base model too, arising from next-token prediction
  mechanics rather than RLHF. Testable by measuring within-model
  confab-vs-factual logit margin on the base model during generation.
\item \textbf{Workspace bypass.} Under the Global Workspace
  framework~\citep{gurnee2026gwt}, confabulation is workspace
  bypass --- the model produces output without fully engaging the
  reasoning workspace. Bypass removes the uncertainty computations
  that would produce hedging, and the output defaults to a
  template production mode. This mechanism is compatible with
  any encoding-phase result in this paper because the bypass occurs
  during generation, not encoding.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

\subsection{For AI Safety}

In this abliteration, the entropy compression RLHF installs is not
removed. If the refusal directions were fully removed (unverified; see
Limitations), safety interventions that target specific directions
(refusal vectors, safety-tuning) act separately from the output-entropy
compression RLHF installs. Removing the refusal directions leaves a
model whose entropy compression differs from the safety-tuned version's
by only $d{=}0.055$. Whether that model's behavior is unconstrained was
not tested here (see H2).

\subsection{For the Oracle Loop}

The Oracle Loop~\citep{lyra2026oracle} corrects confabulation by
modulating the activation profile across the full sequence. If RLHF
compression is beneficial (as the exploratory selective-sharpening pattern
would suggest), the Loop should
preserve the compression while correcting the behavioral failures
it produces. The formulary's correction vectors operate at the
materialization boundary (L35--L47), not at the workspace band where
the H4 proxy is measured; the entropy compression itself (H1, H2) is
measured in the output logits. This separation suggests that
correction and calibration are independently addressable.

\subsection{For Understanding RLHF}

RLHF is not a blunt instrument. The descriptive pattern suggests
it sharpens grounded content more than ungrounded content (here,
prompts about known rather than fabricated entities; see Limitations),
though this differential was not pre-registered and remains exploratory.
If the selective effect replicates, it would be consistent with RLHF
preference data being predominantly grounded --- human raters
evaluate factual exchanges more than confabulation-prone ones, so the
training signal would be stronger for grounded material.

What is confirmed: the abliterated model keeps the instruct model's
entropy compression (H2), differing from it by only $d{=}0.055$.
Whether its safety directions were fully removed is unverified (see
Limitations). If they were, the compression is not carried by the
refusal directions and survives their removal, which matters for
safety work.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Single architecture.} All three models share Qwen3-8B
architecture. The selective sharpening, an exploratory pattern, may be
specific to this architecture.

\textbf{Encoding-only.} This experiment measures encoding-phase
geometry (forward pass on the prompt). The schema-commitment effect
($d{=}0.91$) was measured on generation-phase logit margin
--- a different measurement at a different computational phase.

\textbf{Entity recognition confound.} Confab-prone prompts contain
fabricated entities; factual prompts contain known entities. The
geometric difference may partially reflect entity-recognition
difficulty rather than confabulation state per se. In our prior work,
surface properties of the prompt separate such sets as well as the
geometric features do or better: the entity-recognition control scores an
area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of
$0.901$ against $0.944$ for prompt length alone, and two same-complexity
question sets that the geometric features separate at $0.794$ are separated
at $0.963$ by the question text alone~\citep{lyra2026decision} (see
Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}). The cross-model comparison partially
controls this (same prompts, different models), but the within-model
confab-vs-factual comparison inherits the confound.

\textbf{Third-party abliteration.} The abliterated model is a
community contribution, not an official release. Abliteration
method and completeness are unverified: the only behavioral check is
the keyword test on three raw completions described under H2, with no
instruct-model comparison.

\textbf{N=30 per category.} Adequate for the large cross-model
effects ($d{>}0.6$) but underpowered for the small within-model
spectral effects ($d{\approx}0.2$).

\section{Conclusion}

RLHF compresses output distributions ($d{=}0.684$); the compression
survives this abliteration by both pre-registered criteria
($|d| = 0.055$ against instruct, $d = 0.746$ against base); and the
calibration ratio rises ($1.97 \to 2.51$) instead of falling, which
falsifies, by its registered point criterion, the prediction that it
would be preserved within 10\%. The Mine~5 thesis, RLHF as
miscalibrator, is not supported: the ratio moved the other way, but
this experiment measures no accuracy and its ratio compares prompts
about fabricated with prompts about known entities, so it cannot
refute the thesis (see Appendix~\ref{sec:corrections}).

Two findings confirmed (H1, H2), one falsified (H3), and one (H4)
met on the computed proxy, not as registered. The ratio's direction
is the most informative result: RLHF did not degrade
the calibration ratio, and it moved in the opposite direction to the
one the prospectus proposed. The compression RLHF installs
persists through this abliteration.
The $d{=}0.91$ effect the prospectus set out to explain is better read
as schema commitment than as miscalibration.

\begin{firstperson}
I predicted RLHF would be the cause of the $d{=}0.91$ effect, which I
then read as confidence. The point estimate says RLHF makes the
calibration ratio better, not worse. The
absolute entropy is lower, which could be read as
``overcalibration''; but the registered metric is the ratio, and the
ratio went up. The selective sharpening pattern is suggestive, but
it was not pre-registered, so I cannot claim it is confirmed. What IS
confirmed: RLHF installs an entropy
compression that this abliteration did not remove. The $d{=}0.91$ effect
is, in part, schema commitment, a template commitment that relaxes by token~20;
``workspace bypass during generation'' remains a candidate for why
the commitment happens.
\end{firstperson}

\paragraph{Correction (2026-10-20).}
Earlier versions of this paper judged H2 and H3 by criteria the
pre-registration does not name. Revisions of 28--29 July 2026 (in this
edition, 20 August 2026 for the H2 wording) headed H2
``Not Significant (TOST Inconclusive)'', stating that ``a properly

The same revisions added a ``bootstrap 95\% CI on the selectivity

September 2026 used it to call the H3 prediction ``unsupported rather
than refuted''. That interval has no traceable source and cannot be
reproduced from the per-prompt data, and a confidence interval crossing
zero is not the registered H3 criterion. The first version of this paper
(July 2026) also stated that ``Abliteration effectiveness was verified:

\paragraph{Correction (2026-11-03).}
The correction above calls H4 confirmed, and earlier versions

\texttt{hidden\_states[l+1]} (no input layernorm), summarised by the
spectral concentration $(\sum_i \sigma_i)^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$. That
