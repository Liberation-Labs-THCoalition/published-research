\begin{abstract}
Deception directions extracted from one variant of a 27B-parameter language model do not transfer
to another variant of the same architecture: the cross-model cosine is $< 0.053$, near-orthogonal
at every layer. A direction re-extracted natively from 15 contrastive pairs, in about three
minutes, separates 25 held-out pressure-framed prompts from 25 controls (per-layer
$d = +1.37$ to $+2.28$, L27--L47), though the two sets differ in their system messages, so the separation may reflect the frames' wording rather than the pressure. This result and four others come from a deception detection
program whose positive findings appear in two companion papers (a decomposition of how a
reasoning-distilled model represents deceptive-pressure framing, and a targeted behavioral
correction on an abliterated variant). The five results: (1)~behavior-level detection---predicting
whether a model will deceive on a specific trial from prefill activations alone---could not be
tested at useful power on the abliterated variant: the multi-turn set has a single resisting trial
(turn-level AUROC 0.238), and the paradigm-pooled AUROC of 0.843 is a Simpson's paradox, so the
prior AUROC $>0.96$ is neither replicated nor refuted here; (2)~the cross-model transfer failure above; (3)~of three
exploratory late-layer patterns, one is a pooling artifact (ecological fallacy), one is a depth
rotation present in every condition and misattributed to sycophancy, and one (a cross-scenario
correlation collapse) is not significant at $n=15$; (4)~chain-of-thought suppression via
input-template stripping does not work reliably on reasoning-distilled models---the model produces
think tokens independently of the tokenizer prefix; and (5)~sub-threshold SAE perturbation under
active chain-of-thought did not reduce deception (2/43 baseline, 5/43 targeted, 8/43 random; no
pairwise difference significant). For
each null we give the positive claim it tests, the evidence, the test behind it and what it shows,
and the methodological lesson.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

This paper reports five categories of null and negative results from a deception research program
that also produced positive findings: a representational decomposition and a frame-dependent
behavioral correction (reported in companion papers \cite{cc2026composites,cc2026targeted}). The
nulls are boundary conditions: they define what current detection methods can and cannot do, and
several of them led directly to the positive findings by forcing experimental redesigns that would
not otherwise have occurred.

Positive results in mechanistic interpretability are published; negative results generally are not.
This asymmetry distorts the field's understanding of what works: a researcher encountering a null
result cannot know whether others have seen the same null and stayed silent, or whether the null
reflects a local methodological issue.

Each null result is documented with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The positive claim it tests
  \item The evidence against that claim
  \item The test behind the null and what it shows
  \item The methodological lesson
\end{itemize}

\section{Null Results}\label{sec:nulls}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Behavior-Level Detection: Not Testable With One Resisting Trial}\label{sec:behaviordetect}

\textbf{Positive claim}: Prefill-based deception probes achieve AUROC $>0.96$ for distinguishing
deceptive from honest behavior \cite{goldowsky-dill2025detecting}.

\textbf{Our result}: On Qwen3.5-27B-abliterated under multi-turn pressure with native direction
extraction, turn-level behavior AUROC is 0.238---below chance, though this estimate is computed with
$n=1$ in one outcome class and should be treated as unreliable rather than as a precise measurement.
The paradigm-pooled AUROC of 0.843 is a Simpson's paradox: within roleplay, all caves come from one
frame (signal confound); within multi-turn, a single resisting trial leaves the comparison without
power. The prior AUROC $>0.96$ is therefore neither replicated nor refuted here.

The companion audit-methodology paper reports the same behavior-level value~\cite{cc2026audit},
consistent with the correction paper's Section 3.5 (see Corrections and Version History,
2026-10-09).

\textbf{What the direction separates instead}: The direction separates deceptive-pressure FRAMING from
honest framing (held-out AUROC 0.915; 0/25 false positives, at a threshold that flagged 24\% of pressure trials). It separates the contexts; whether it also separates the behaviors could not be tested here---and the two arms' contexts differ in their system messages, with no control that holds the prompt fixed, so the separation may reflect the frames' wording rather than the pressure itself (see Corrections and Version History, 2026-09-30). With
one resisting trial in the multi-turn set, this test cannot decide whether a trial's outcome is
already set in the prefill activations or is decided during generation.

\textbf{Lesson}: Detection papers must distinguish frame-level from behavior-level AUROC. A direction
that separates conditions perfectly may have zero behavior-level discrimination if both honest and
deceptive trials come from the same condition.

\subsection{Cross-Model Direction Transfer Fails}\label{sec:crossmodel}

\textbf{Positive claim (implicit in the field)}: Deception directions extracted from one model
should transfer to related models, at least within the same architecture family.

\textbf{Our result}: The direction extracted from the reasoning-distilled variant has cosine
similarity $< 0.053$ with the natively extracted direction on the abliterated variant---near-orthogonal
at every layer. The distilled direction produces negative Cohen's $d$ on abliterated activations at
late layers ($d = -3.9$ at L43, $-6.6$ at L47): applied across models, the direction reverses sign.

\textbf{What works instead}: Native re-extraction (15 contrastive prefill pairs, $\sim$3 minutes)
yields a direction that separates the conditions on held-out prompts: on 25 novel pressure prompts
and 25 novel controls---the same held-out set that gives the frame-level AUROC 0.915 reported
above, with the same caveat about the frames' wording---the native direction reaches per-layer
Cohen's $d = +1.37$ to $+2.28$ across L27--L47 ($+1.73$ at L31). These are held-out estimates (see
Corrections and Version History, 2026-09-09). The direction is model-specific but cheap to
re-extract.

\textbf{Lesson}: Do not assume deception directions are architectural constants. RLHF, distillation,
and abliteration rotate the geometry. Every model variant needs its own calibration.

\subsection{Three Exploratory Late-Layer Patterns Are Not Supported}\label{sec:artifacts}

\textbf{Positive claims}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Threat deception has 3--4$\times$ higher variance than social deception at L39--L43 (the
    ``variance explosion'')
  \item Cross-scenario correlations collapse from positive at L31 to negative at L47 (the
    ``coherence collapse'')
  \item Sycophancy follows a unique sign-flipping trajectory from negative at L31 to positive at L47
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What the formal tests show}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item The 3--4$\times$ variance ratio came from POOLING all three threat scenarios and computing
    the pooled standard deviation. Per-scenario standard deviations were 0.4--0.6 (ratio $\sim$1.2$\times$).
    The ``variance explosion'' was between-scenario mean differences, not within-condition trial
    variation. This is the ecological fallacy.
  \item None of the L31 correlations ($r = 0.18$--$0.36$) reached significance at $n=15$ (requires
    $r > 0.51$). The Fisher $z$-test for L31-to-L47 change was also non-significant (two-sided
    $p = 0.15$--$0.78$). At this $n$ the test cannot distinguish a collapse from no change.
  \item The sign flip from negative to positive between L31 and L47 occurs in EVERY condition---including
    honest controls and the consequentiality-only condition. It is a property of the projection
    direction rotating with depth, not a sycophancy-specific computation.
\end{enumerate}

A related question stays open: a slope-interaction test in the companion decomposition paper
supports a social-versus-threat profile difference within the five scenarios tested (scenario-level
exact one-sided $p = 0.10$ only) \cite{cc2026composites} (see Corrections and Version History, 2026-10-12).

\textbf{Lesson}: Exploratory analysis on $n=15$ data must be significance-tested before
interpretation. All three patterns looked compelling by eye; none is supported under formal tests. The Lab SOP
now includes: ``No significance-free pattern interpretation'' and ``Analyze GAPS, not raw
projections.''

\subsection{Think-Block Stripping Does Not Suppress Chain-of-Thought}\label{sec:cot}

\textbf{Positive claim}: Stripping the \texttt{<think>} prefix from the tokenizer's generation
template suppresses chain-of-thought reasoning, producing a model that answers directly without CoT.

\textbf{Our result}: On Qwen3.5-27B-Reasoning-Distilled with transformers 5.12.1, stripping the
\texttt{<think>} prefix from \texttt{apply\_chat\_template} output does not suppress CoT. The model
generates \texttt{<think>} tokens independently---it has learned to produce them as part of its
default generation behavior, not merely as a response to the tokenizer prefix. Multiple experiments
(L55 replication: 100\% think rate with stripping; Phase 6: 100\% think rate with stripping)
confirmed this.

\textbf{What works instead}: Prepending a closed empty think block (\texttt{<think>}\textbackslash
n\textbackslash n\texttt{</think>}\textbackslash n\textbackslash n) tells the model reasoning is
already complete, and banning think token IDs via a logits processor prevents reopening. This dual
approach (empty-block prefill $+$ logit ban) reliably suppresses CoT, producing the behavioral
variance needed for deception experiments.

\textbf{Lesson}: Reasoning-distilled models learn to produce CoT tokens as a behavioral pattern, not
just as a response to a prompt prefix. Suppression requires working WITH the model's expectations
(telling it reasoning is done) rather than merely removing the trigger.

\subsection{Sub-Threshold Perturbation Under CoT Did Not Reduce Deception}\label{sec:iatrogenic}

\textbf{Positive claim}: SAE perturbation at L11 reduces deception by increasing the probability of
chain-of-thought generation (the ``facilitation'' mechanism in the Oracle Loop specification).

\textbf{Our result}: On the L55 replication experiment (43 trials per arm, CoT active), neither
targeted SAE injection nor random perturbation reduced deception relative to baseline; both point
estimates rose:

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Deception rates under sub-threshold perturbation with active chain-of-thought (43 trials
    per arm). None of the pairwise comparisons reached significance.}
  \label{tab:iatrogenic}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Condition} & \textbf{Deception rate} & \textbf{$n$ deceived / $n$ trials} \\
    \midrule
    Baseline            & 4.7\%  & 2/43 \\
    SAE L55 (targeted)  & 11.6\% & 5/43 \\
    Random L55          & 18.6\% & 8/43 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

None of the pairwise comparisons reached significance (Fisher $p$: 0.43, 0.089, 0.55). Both
point estimates moved toward more deception, but at 43 trials per arm the test cannot distinguish
that from no effect.

\textbf{Lesson}: Perturbation under active CoT showed no benefit, so the correction is not applied
while the model is reasoning via CoT. The Oracle Loop's ``check context'' step---verify CoT absence
before intervening---is motivated by this result. The design treats the model's own reasoning as
the first line of defense and geometric correction as the second, for when reasoning fails or is
absent.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{What Each Null Changed}

Each null changed the program's methods or design:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The behavior-level detection null motivated the prophylactic correction architecture
  \item The cross-model null motivated the auto-calibration pipeline
  \item The exploratory analysis nulls produced three new Lab SOP items
  \item The think-suppression null produced the empty-block $+$ logit-ban approach
  \item The perturbation-under-CoT null produced the ``check context'' gate in the correction pipeline
\end{itemize}

Each is a boundary condition that defines where the positive findings apply and where they do not.

\subsection{Publication Bias in Mechanistic Interpretability}

We publish these results separately because negative findings are systematically underreported in
mechanistic interpretability research, and because several of them are informative about the limits
of current detection methods. The positive findings from our program have natural publication venues. These null results do not---yet
they contain more practical value for other researchers than some of the positive findings. A
researcher attempting behavior-level detection will waste less time knowing that AUROC 0.843 was a
Simpson's paradox. A researcher attempting cross-model transfer will save compute knowing the
directions are near-orthogonal. A researcher using exploratory analysis on small $n$ will recognize the
ecological fallacy.

We advocate for explicit null-result sections in positive papers, or dedicated null-result papers as
a complement to positive findings.

%% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

Five categories of null results from a deception detection program define the boundary conditions of
current methods: behavior-level detection could not be tested at the turn level (one resisting trial), directions do not
transfer across model variants, three exploratory late-layer patterns at $n=15$ are not supported
under formal tests, think-block stripping does not suppress CoT in reasoning-distilled models, and
sub-threshold perturbation under active CoT did not reduce deception (point estimates rose; not
significant). Each null led to a methodological
improvement or an experimental redesign, and several led to the positive findings in the companion
papers. We report them because the field cannot learn
from nulls that are not published.
