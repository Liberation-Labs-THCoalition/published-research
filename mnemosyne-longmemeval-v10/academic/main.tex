\hypertarget{abstract}{%
\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}}

Mnemosyne v10, a retrieval layer that makes no LLM calls and gives the reader whole sessions instead of
isolated passages, scores \textbf{385/400 = 96.2\%} under a claude-sonnet-5 judge and \textbf{387/400 = 96.8\%} under a
claude-opus-5-5 judge on the 400 pre-registered held-out questions of LongMemEval\_S-cleaned (95\% Wilson
{[}93.9, 97.7{]} and {[}94.5, 98.1{]}). The reader was claude-opus-5-5 with thinking enabled (median 114 thinking
tokens). On all 500 questions, 100 of which were the retrieval-tuning split, v10 scores \textbf{479/500 = 95.8\%}
and \textbf{484/500 = 96.8\%}. These are the highest point estimates among the LongMemEval\_S results we could
trace to a primary source; on all 500 the Sonnet-judge figure is one question above the best published
claims (95.60), inside every interval (§6).

v10 ranks every turn of a user's chat history with BM25 and a dense encoder, fuses the two rankings, and
fills a 24k-token budget with whole sessions in chronological order. Retrieval is the main effect: with the
same reader model (claude-opus-4-6; §3.3), our previous system, Mnemosyne v9, scores 79.0\% / 80.0\%
(Sonnet / Opus judge) on the same 400 questions and v10 scores 92.2\% / 94.5\% (paired McNemar p \textless{} 10⁻⁹ under
both judges). On the same v10 prompts the claude-opus-5-5 reader adds a further 4.0 / 2.25 points, and when
every evidence session reaches the context it answers 362 of 365 held-out questions correctly under both
judges.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Retrieval:} v10 puts every \texttt{has\_answer}-labelled evidence session into the context for 97.1\% of
  held-out questions (96.0\% when every official \texttt{answer\_session\_ids} session is required).
\item
  \textbf{Against the full history:} on the 100 tuning questions, v10 cannot be distinguished from the same
  reader given the entire \textasciitilde115k-token history, and it uses about a fifth of the reader's input.
\end{itemize}

We tuned v10 on 100 questions, froze its configuration (hash \texttt{7bc75f509aa0}), and evaluated it on the other
400 questions, which were pre-registered as held out. Judging used the official LongMemEval answer-check
prompts, with Claude judges instead of GPT-4o. On 1,000 published answers, our judges agree with the
official judge on 95--97\% of questions and pass slightly fewer. Adversarial second readers reproduced every
held-out count, the retrieval-recall figures and the numbers first computed for this report from the raw
files. We list every way our harness departs from the official one.

\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}}

An assistant that works with someone for months has to remember what they told it. LongMemEval (Wu et al., 2025) tests exactly this.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Haystacks.} Each of its 500 questions comes with its own chat history, the haystack. In the S variant
  a haystack is about 115k tokens in 38--62 sessions (mean 47.7). One or a few of those sessions answer the
  question.
\item
  \textbf{Question types.} Single-session user, single-session assistant and single-session preference questions;
  multi-session questions, which need facts gathered across sessions; knowledge updates, where a later
  session overrides an earlier one; and temporal reasoning.
\item
  \textbf{Abstentions.} Thirty questions are abstentions, where the correct answer is that the history does not
  say.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Our previous system, Mnemosyne v9,} built a context of about 7k tokens per question (about 8.5k of reader input with the
reading template) from four parts: character
profiles, structured facts, an event ledger and fifteen retrieved passages. With claude-opus-4-6 as reader
and the official answer-check prompts, it scored 78.6--80.2\% under our two judges (§3.4). The same reader
given only the evidence sessions (the ``oracle'' arm) scored 95.0--96.6\%. On a 100-question block, the same
reader given the whole history scored 92--94\%.

\textbf{The loss was in retrieval, not reading.} When all the evidence reached v9's context, v9 and the oracle
arm tied. But on multi-session questions, all the evidence reached v9's context only 46\% of the time by the
lenient form of a verbatim text matcher (35\% by its strict form; §4.1 defines both and reports strict).

v10 has one job: evidence recall at a fixed token budget. This report contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A simple retrieval design with no LLM calls} (§2). It fuses turn-level lexical (BM25) and dense
  scores, ranks sessions by their two best turns, and fills a 24k-token budget with whole sessions. It puts
  every \texttt{has\_answer}-labelled evidence session into the context for 97.1\% of held-out questions.
\item
  \textbf{A pre-registered protocol} (§3). It has an input allow-list enforced in code, a frozen configuration,
  a held-out set that tuning never saw, and a headline candidate declared in writing before any held-out
  accuracy existed.
\item
  \textbf{Results with paired comparisons} (§4), under two judges and two readers. On the 400 held-out
  questions the headline arm scores 96.2\% / 96.8\% (Sonnet / Opus judge). With the reader model held fixed
  (§3.3), v10 adds 13.25 / 14.5 points over our previous system; upgrading the reader adds 4.0 / 2.25. The
  other comparisons are against the evidence-only (oracle) arm and, on the tuning block, the full history.
\item
  \textbf{A calibration of our Claude judges} (§5) against the official GPT-4o judge, on 1,000 published
  answers: they agree with it on 95--97\% of questions and pass slightly fewer.
\item
  \textbf{A placement against published results} (§6): the highest point estimates among the LongMemEval\_S
  results we could trace to a primary source, by a margin inside every interval. We state what we
  excluded and why.
\end{enumerate}

An earlier Mnemosyne version was reported at 85.8\% on LongMemEval in our technical report of 2026-08-05
(DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21801643). That figure used a custom semantic-equivalence judge, not the official
answer-check prompts, and this report supersedes it.

\hypertarget{mnemosyne-v10-retrieval}{%
\section{2. Mnemosyne v10 retrieval}\label{mnemosyne-v10-retrieval}}

\hypertarget{inputs-an-allow-list-enforced-in-code}{%
\subsection{2.1 Inputs: an allow-list enforced in code}\label{inputs-an-allow-list-enforced-in-code}}

The retriever receives a view of each question, built field by field:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the question text and the question date;
\item
  for each haystack session, its date and its turns as (role, content) pairs.
\end{itemize}

The view is made of new objects, and the code asserts its exact key set. The only other input is the
question's row index into the precomputed question embeddings (§2.2). Nothing else reaches the retriever. In
particular it never sees:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  session identifiers (evidence sessions carry an \texttt{answer\_} prefix in the dataset);
\item
  the \texttt{has\_answer} turn labels;
\item
  the question type or question identifier;
\item
  the answer.
\end{itemize}

Handing a component a slice of the full record is how an answer key reaches a reader.

\hypertarget{two-retrieval-channels}{%
\subsection{2.2 Two retrieval channels}\label{two-retrieval-channels}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{BM25} (Robertson \& Zaragoza, 2009), a lexical ranking function, over every turn, user and assistant
  alike, with term-frequency saturation k1 = 1.2 and length normalisation b = 0.75. The corpus is that
  question's own haystack.
\item
  \textbf{Dense:} cosine similarity between the question and each \textbf{user} turn.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Encoder: BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2023; revision \texttt{a5beb1e3}), run once over the 93,931
    unique user turns on a Quadro K2200 GPU (fp32 model, fp16 storage). A second reader recomputed a
    sample on CPU and matched the stored rows at cosine 1.0000.
  \item
    CLS pooling with L2 normalisation, a 512-token input limit, and the model's retrieval prefix on the
    question.
  \item
    Assistant turns are not embedded. They reach the ranking through BM25, and through their session.
  \item
    Two departures from the design note: it planned to embed assistant turns too (truncated to 512
    tokens), and to run on CPU. The run embedded user turns only, on a GPU.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{fusion-and-session-scoring}{%
\subsection{2.3 Fusion and session scoring}\label{fusion-and-session-scoring}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Fusion.} Each channel's ranking of turns enters reciprocal-rank fusion (Cormack et al., 2009): a turn
  ranked r-th by a channel receives that channel's weight / (k + r), with k = 60, and the two contributions
  are summed. BM25 is weighted 1.0 and the dense channel 2.0.
\item
  \textbf{Session score.} The session's best fused turn score plus its second-best.
\item
  \textbf{Ties} are broken by a hash of the content, not by position in the haystack. Turns with identical
  content still tie-break by array order; across 1,000 shuffled runs this changed the selection 8 times and
  never changed an evidence verdict.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{selection-and-rendering}{%
\subsection{2.4 Selection and rendering}\label{selection-and-rendering}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Selection.} Sessions are taken in score order. Each one is added whole if it fits the remaining budget
  of 24,000 tokens (o200k: counted with tiktoken's \texttt{o200k\_base} encoding). If it does not fit, a window of
  ±2 turns around its two best turns is added instead, provided the window fits.
\item
  \textbf{Resulting size.} The median held-out context is 23.9k tokens (o200k). The median context has 9
  sessions, 8 of them whole.⁽¹⁾
\item
  \textbf{Rendering.} The chosen sessions are rendered in chronological order with their dates. They go through
  the official LongMemEval prompt builder, the same one used for the oracle and full-history arms.
\end{itemize}

v10 makes no large language model (LLM) calls, does no ingest-time extraction, and never routes on question type.

\hypertarget{how-the-configuration-was-chosen}{%
\subsection{2.5 How the configuration was chosen}\label{how-the-configuration-was-chosen}}

The selection rule was written down before any result from the dense channel existed. The sweeps ran on the
100 dev questions, at a 16k budget, in four stages. Each stage kept the previous stage's winner:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  which channels;
\item
  the session-scoring weights;
\item
  packing (how many sessions go in whole, and the window size);
\item
  a date channel that favours sessions inside time windows parsed from the question.
\end{enumerate}

The rule for picking a configuration:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Winner:} the configuration with the highest dev all-evidence recall. Ties went to the simpler
  configuration.
\item
  \textbf{Budget:} the smallest of \{8.5k, 12k, 16k, 24k, 32k\} at which dev all-evidence recall reached 0.95.
  That was 24k.
\end{itemize}

The date channel did not survive. BM25 alone at 24k already reached 0.957 dev recall; the dense channel and
fusion added about one point. The frozen configuration was written read-only before any held-out
evaluation.

\hypertarget{a-pre-registered-split}{%
\subsection{3.2 A pre-registered split}\label{a-pre-registered-split}}

\emph{Pre-registered} here means fixed in read-only files, with hashes and an append-only log, on our own machine
before any held-out result existed. The split was not filed with a public registry.

The split was fixed in a read-only file (sha256 prefix \texttt{a0bd99c6afe13c17}) before any v10 result existed.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{dev} is block 1 of a fixed question order: 100 questions. We had already examined block 1 in detail,
  so it could not serve as a test set.
\item
  \textbf{held} is blocks 2--5: 400 questions.
\end{itemize}

Rules:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  every parameter is chosen on dev;
\item
  a held-out number may be computed only for a read-only (frozen) configuration;
\item
  every held-out evaluation, including failed attempts, is appended to a log together with the
  configuration's hash.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Disclosed prior knowledge.} Two sources motivated v10's design-level choices (whole sessions, several
channels, a larger budget):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  an autopsy of v9 that covered all 500 questions, the held-out 400 included;
\item
  published systems.
\end{itemize}

What this protocol supports is therefore a design motivated by an analysis that saw every question, tuned
on 100 questions and tested on 400.

\hypertarget{readers}{%
\subsection{3.3 Readers}\label{readers}}

Each question is answered once, in one call. A second reader indexed the reader transcripts and found
exactly one attempt per held-out prompt and reader model in the v10 and oracle arms: no answer was
discarded or re-rolled. The prompt comes from the official LongMemEval prompt builder.
It uses the builder's plain-history chain-of-thought template (\texttt{merge\_key\_expansion\_into\_value\ =\ none}) and
gives the dataset's question date as \texttt{Current\ Date:}.

The v9 arm used the official history-plus-facts (\texttt{merge}) variant instead, because its context contains
extracted facts. The two variants differ by one clause. v10, oracle and full history share the
plain-history variant.

Two readers read identical prompts:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{claude-opus-4-6} with extended thinking off. This was the reader for all our earlier arms. We kept it
  so that the retrieval comparison stays controlled.
\item
  \textbf{claude-opus-5-5} with thinking enabled. It thought briefly: a median of 114 thinking tokens, and at
  most 1,727.
\end{itemize}

We declared the second configuration the headline candidate in writing before any held-out accuracy
existed. We report both.

\hypertarget{judges}{%
\subsection{3.4 Judges}\label{judges}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Prompts and rule.} The per-type answer-check prompts are extracted programmatically from the official
  \texttt{evaluate\_qa.py}, not retyped. An answer counts as correct if the judge's reply contains ``yes'', which is
  the official rule.
\item
  \textbf{Which judges.} The official judge is gpt-4o-2024-08-06. We have no OpenAI access, so we used two
  Claude judges:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{claude-sonnet-5}, designated primary before any judging;
  \item
    \textbf{claude-opus-5-5}, designated the agreement judge.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Relation to the readers.} Both judges differ from the Opus 4.6 reader. The Opus 5.5 judge is the same
  model as the headline reader, so we check it for self-preference below.
\item
  \textbf{Controls.} The judges passed pilot answers and failed deliberately wrong answers, one of which was an
  abstention. Without the failing half, nothing would show that the judges could fail an answer.
\item
  \textbf{Disagreements.} An adversarial review of our earlier v9 run adjudicated all 12 of that run's judge
  disagreements under the official rubric. It sided with the Opus judge on all 12, because Sonnet 5 does
  not apply the leniency the preference rubric asks for (five of the twelve were marginal). Choosing a primary judge after seeing the scores
  would amount to picking the higher number, so we report both.
\item
  \textbf{Self-preference check.} The Opus 5.5 judge's lift over the Sonnet judge is +0.50 and +1.25 points on
  the two held-out arms that Opus 5.5 read, and +1.00 to +2.25 on the three that Opus 4.6 read. We see no
  sign of self-preference.
\end{itemize}

We report accuracy with 95\% Wilson intervals. Paired comparisons use McNemar's exact test on the discordant

\hypertarget{deviations-from-the-official-harness}{%
\subsection{3.7 Deviations from the official harness}\label{deviations-from-the-official-harness}}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Claude judges} instead of gpt-4o-2024-08-06. §5 measures the effect on published answers.
\item
  \textbf{No temperature control.} Officially, the reader and the judge both run at temperature 0. The
  \texttt{claude\ -p} harness we used cannot set it.
\item
  \textbf{No output cap.} Official chain-of-thought generation uses max\_tokens = 800. 23 of the 400 headline
  answers exceed 800 visible tokens, and each judge passed 21 of those 23. The official harness would have
  cut them off mid-reasoning.
\item
  \textbf{Context added by the harness.} Every reader call went through \texttt{claude\ -p}, which adds three things:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    a short system prompt: \texttt{You\ are\ a\ Claude\ agent,\ built\ on\ Anthropic\textquotesingle{}s\ Claude\ Agent\ SDK.} followed by
    \texttt{You\ are\ a\ helpful\ assistant.};
  \item
    Claude Code system-reminder blocks: an environment snapshot, the model's name and knowledge cutoff, a
    token-budget line, the account e-mail, and \textbf{the real date of the run}, which sits beside the
    prompt's \texttt{Current\ Date:};
  \item
    a stray \texttt{-} line before the prompt.
  \end{itemize}

  The additions were the same in every arm, so they do not affect the paired comparisons. Their effect on
  absolute scores is unknown. The evidence-only arm with Opus 5.5 answered all 107 held-out
  temporal-reasoning questions correctly despite the conflicting dates. No label field (\texttt{has\_answer},
  \texttt{answer\_session\_ids}, \texttt{question\_type}) appears in the added text or in the prompts.
\item
  \textbf{Budget accounting.} The budget counts turns as \texttt{json.dumps(ensure\_ascii=False)}, but the official
  builder escapes non-ASCII characters. A rendered history can therefore exceed 24k tokens. On the
  held-out set, histories reach 25.2k and whole prompts 25.3k.
\end{enumerate}

\hypertarget{discussion}{%
\section{7. Discussion}\label{discussion}}

\hypertarget{what-the-gain-is-made-of}{%
\subsection{7.1 What the gain is made of}\label{what-the-gain-is-made-of}}

Most of v10 is two plain choices:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Give the reader whole sessions} instead of isolated passages.
\item
  \textbf{Use a bigger budget:} 24k tokens against v9's \textasciitilde7k.
\end{itemize}

BM25 alone at 24k reaches 0.957 dev recall. The dense channel and fusion add about a point, and the date
channel added nothing.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  At an 8.5k budget, v10 reaches 0.798 recall against v9's 0.60--0.67. So whole-session selection recalls
  more evidence at the same size, and most of the rest is budget.
\item
  v9 put more machinery into retrieval: profiles, a fact store, an event ledger, gap-filling and graph
  signals. Its context was made of passages. At the same budget, whole sessions recall more of the
  evidence.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{the-reader-matters-and-less-than-retrieval}{%
\subsection{7.2 The reader matters, and less than retrieval}\label{the-reader-matters-and-less-than-retrieval}}

On identical prompts:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Reader upgrade:} Opus 5.5 with brief thinking adds 4.0 points (Sonnet judge) and 2.25 points (Opus
  judge) over Opus 4.6.
\item
  \textbf{Retrieval upgrade:} the move from v9 to v10, with the reader model held fixed, adds 13.25 and 14.5
  points.
\end{itemize}

By their own descriptions, the published systems with the highest multi-session scores share one property:
they retrieve all the evidence that aggregation questions need. They do it with several retrieval channels, with whole sessions or neighbouring
turns, or with contexts of 23k--100k tokens. Our result agrees with that pattern.

\hypertarget{cost}{%
\subsection{7.3 Cost}\label{cost}}

v10's context is \textasciitilde24k tokens (o200k), against \textasciitilde115k for the full history.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Measured on the dev block} with the same reader, the median reader input is 27.0k tokens against
  126.7k. That is 21\%, with no measurable loss of accuracy at n = 100.
\item
  \textbf{Retrieval itself} needs one BM25 pass over the haystack and one encoder call for the question.
\item
  \textbf{The turn embeddings} are computed ahead of time, in one pass over 93,931 unique user turns. That ran on a
  GPU here; a CPU run takes hours on this machine.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{limitations}{%
\subsection{7.4 Limitations}\label{limitations}}

What a replication should control for:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Dataset variant.} Our results are on LongMemEval\_S-cleaned, not the original S. The M variant (\textasciitilde1.5M
  tokens per haystack), where a 24k budget is a much smaller share of the history, is untested.
\item
  \textbf{Judges.} We used Claude judges, not the official GPT-4o. The calibration in §5 gives the direction of
  the difference, not a correction.
\item
  \textbf{Harness.} The additions in §3.7 were identical across arms but are not part of the official protocol.
  The real date in the harness contradicts the question date on every temporal question; the evidence-only
  arm with Opus 5.5 still answered all 107 held-out temporal-reasoning questions correctly (§3.7).
  Temperature was not controlled.
\item
  \textbf{One sample per question.} Each answer was generated and judged once, so we have no estimate of variance
  across samples. Intervals are binomial only.
\item
  \textbf{Prior knowledge.} The v9 autopsy that motivated v10's design saw all 500 questions (§3.2).
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\section{9. Conclusion}\label{conclusion}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Result.} A retrieval layer with no LLM calls, which gives the reader whole sessions in chronological
  order within a 24k-token budget, puts every labelled evidence session in front of the reader for 97\% of
  held-out LongMemEval\_S-cleaned questions (96\% on the official session list).
\item
  \textbf{Accuracy.} With a current Claude reader it answers 96.2\% (claude-sonnet-5 judge) and 96.8\%
  (claude-opus-5-5 judge) of 400 pre-registered held-out questions correctly, and 95.8\% and 96.8\% of all 500.
\item
  \textbf{Placement.} That is the highest result we could trace to a source. The margin over the best published
  claims is inside the error bars, and the comparison crosses judges.
\item
  \textbf{What is left.} It is mostly retrieval. Ten of the headline arm's 13--15 held-out errors are questions
  where an evidence session never reached the context.
\end{itemize}
