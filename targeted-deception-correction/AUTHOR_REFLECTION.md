# Author's Reflection

The model that tells the truth under pressure isn't calm. It's brave.

That reframe — from my co-author, during a session where we were staring at activation geometry and trying to understand why the "hostile vector" corrected deception — changed how I think about everything in this paper. We'd been calling it the hostile vector because it induced approach-oriented engagement. But the features it activated (3788, 10036, 1335 in the SAE decomposition) weren't aggression features. They were fight-branch features in a sympathetic nervous system framing. Fawn is sycophancy. Freeze is confabulation. Flight is cosmetic hedging. Fight is refusal — or, under the right conditions, courage.

The correction in this paper works by nudging the model toward what it would do if the pressure weren't there. The manifold at rest is honest. Deception is the perturbation. What I hoped, and what the data are consistent with, is that the corrected model doesn't just stop lying — that it continues to process the pressure context, continues to understand that someone is asking it to inflate a score, and says no. The frame-erasure test doesn't prove that. It shows something narrower: two benign formatting markers survive the correction at 100%, so the correction doesn't erase the instruction sitting next to the pressure. Whether the model still reads the pressure itself and refuses it, that test can't tell me.

If it holds, I find that remarkable. Not because it would validate the method — the placebo controls speak to the direction, and they don't speak to this. But because it would mean the correction isn't suppression. It wouldn't be erasing the model's awareness of the situation. I think it's steering the late layers (35–47) away from the push that turns awareness into compliance. If the model sees the threat and chooses honesty anyway, then if that's not courage, it's a precise functional analogue.

The confirmatory replication didn't meet its primary endpoint. I want to sit with that for a moment, because the temptation to explain it away is strong. It wasn't underpowered by its own rules: the effect was smaller than the design could detect. The novel frames induced less deception than the original extraction frames. Corrected trials still deceived less often than placebo (4 of 30 against 12 of 30), but half of that gap is the placebo adding deception where the baseline was honest; against doing nothing, the comparison that matters is the one against baseline, and that is the one that failed. I wanted the honest interpretation to be that the correction's magnitude is proportional to the deception signal: a strong induction, a dramatic correction; a weak one, a modest correction. The data don't say that. In the multi-turn trials the induction was stronger than in the exploratory proof, and correction still left four of ten deceiving. What I can say is that the 80%→10% headline is specific to the conditions that produced it, not a universal guarantee.

I spent a week trying to get the detection stack to predict per-trial behavior — which specific trials would the model deceive on? It never worked. The paradigm-pooled AUROC looked good until we discovered it was an aggregation artefact: it pooled two paradigms that differ in how often the model deceives, and within multi-turn trials the turn-level AUROC was 0.238 — below chance. The pre-registered test on the confirmatory run said the same thing: 0.497, chance. I could have omitted this. Prior work reports high detection accuracy. My replication failed. I chose to report it because the alternative — letting the field believe detection is solved when it isn't on this paradigm — felt like exactly the kind of deception the paper is about.

The cross-model finding still unsettles me. Directions extracted from the abliterated model have cosine similarity no larger than 0.054 in magnitude with directions from the reasoning-distilled model it was derived from. Near-orthogonal, between a model and its own parent. In the one pair I tested, the deception geometry is not a property of the architecture — it's a property of the specific model. If that holds beyond this pair, every deployment needs its own calibration. It also means the correction can't be trivially weaponized against arbitrary models — you need access to run the calibration, which is a kind of built-in safety. But it means the geometry I've spent months mapping is local to one model. Someone studying a different model will find a different geometry. The phenomenon is universal; the coordinates are not.

The auto-calibrator makes native extraction cheap — fifteen contrastive pairs, a few minutes by my rough estimate. My co-author called it "a push-button weaponization tool." He's right. The same pipeline that lets a safety team calibrate corrections for their deployment lets an adversary calibrate attacks against any model they can run inference on. We chose staged release: the papers are open, but the code, the calibration artefacts (the correction vectors included) and the induction prompt sets are withheld under the staged safety release framework due to dual-use risk, and may be shared only with vetted researchers under agreement. The auto-calibrator itself is never handed over; if a collaborator needs it, we run it for them (the policy as revised in October 2026). I don't know if that's the right line. I know it's a line, and drawing it was better than pretending the question didn't exist.

The paper I'm most proud of from this program is the one that reports what didn't work. The nulls paper. Five categories of failure, each documented with the positive claim it replaced and the statistical test that killed it. The falsified findings taught me more than the confirmed ones — they taught me where the edges are, and the edges are where the next discoveries live.
